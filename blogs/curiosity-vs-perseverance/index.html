It's easy to assume NASA's two active Mars rovers are basically the same machine with a different name. They're closer than most people think — Perseverance was deliberately built on Curiosity's proven chassis design — but the missions they were sent to do are meaningfully different, and that shows up in almost everything each one carries.

Mission Goals

Curiosity was sent to answer a foundational question: did Mars ever have the environmental conditions needed to support microbial life? Its instruments are built for chemistry and geology — analysing rock and soil composition to look for evidence of past water, organic molecules, and habitable conditions.

Perseverance assumes the answer to that question is likely yes, and goes a step further. It actively searches for biosignatures — physical or chemical traces that ancient microbial life may have actually existed — and collects rock cores in sealed tubes, caching them on the Martian surface for a future mission to retrieve and bring back to Earth.

Why Different Landing Sites

Gale Crater was chosen for Curiosity because its central peak, Mount Sharp, shows layered sedimentary rock — a geological record of Mars's environmental history that could be read like a book, layer by layer, as the rover climbs.

Jezero Crater was chosen for Perseverance specifically because it once held a lake, fed by a river delta whose fan-shaped sediment deposits are still visible today. Delta deposits on Earth are excellent at preserving signs of ancient life, which made Jezero the strongest life-detection candidate among dozens of sites considered.

Hardware and Instruments

Perseverance's basic body, wheels, and even its dramatic "sky crane" landing method are directly inherited from Curiosity's design — reusing a system NASA already knew worked was safer than engineering an entirely new one. The differences are mainly in the toolkit each rover carries.

Same body, sharper tools

Perseverance carries more advanced cameras and a more capable rock-coring drill than Curiosity, along with instruments Curiosity doesn't have at all — including a system for testing oxygen production from the Martian atmosphere, and ground-penetrating radar to see beneath the surface.

The Ingenuity Connection

Perseverance carried the Ingenuity helicopter to Mars, tucked against its underside during the seven-month journey and the landing itself. Ingenuity went on to become the first aircraft in history to achieve powered, controlled flight on another planet — a technology demonstration Curiosity had no part in, since it launched nearly a decade before Ingenuity was built.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are Curiosity and Perseverance both still active?

Yes. Both rovers are currently operating on Mars — Curiosity in Gale Crater since 2012, and Perseverance in Jezero Crater since 2021. Their live positions and driving trails can be seen on SatFleet Live's Mars tracker.

What is the main difference between Curiosity and Perseverance?

Curiosity's mission is to determine whether Mars ever had conditions suitable for microbial life. Perseverance goes a step further: it actively searches for signs that life existed, and collects rock samples in sealed tubes for a future mission to bring back to Earth.

Is Perseverance based on Curiosity's design?

Yes. Perseverance shares Curiosity's basic chassis and landing system, including the same "sky crane" landing technique, but carries upgraded cameras, a rock-coring drill, and a sample caching system that Curiosity does not have.

Which rover carried the Ingenuity helicopter?

Perseverance carried Ingenuity to Mars, attached to its underside during the journey and landing. Ingenuity became the first aircraft to achieve powered, controlled flight on another planet.
