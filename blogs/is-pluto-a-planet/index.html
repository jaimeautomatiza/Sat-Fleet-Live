Is Pluto a Planet?

✕ No — reclassified in 2006

Not because it shrank, and not because astronomers changed their minds about Pluto specifically. The definition of "planet" changed underneath it — here's exactly why.

For 76 years, from its discovery in 1930 until 2006, Pluto was the solar system's ninth planet without question. Then, in a single vote at a conference in Prague, it wasn't anymore. Pluto itself never changed — what changed was the definition of the word "planet," and Pluto was the object that happened to fall on the wrong side of the new line.

The Three Rules a Planet Must Meet

In 2006, the International Astronomical Union (IAU) formally defined "planet" for the first time, using three criteria. To qualify, an object must:

✓
Orbit the SunPluto passes this easily — it has a well-defined, stable orbit around the Sun.
✓
Be round due to its own gravityPluto passes this too — it's massive enough that gravity has pulled it into a spherical shape.
✕
Have "cleared its neighborhood"Pluto fails here. It orbits within the Kuiper Belt, a region packed with thousands of similar icy objects, some nearly its own size — it hasn't gravitationally cleared or dominated that region the way Earth, Jupiter, or any of the 8 planets have theirs.

Objects that meet the first two criteria but not the third were placed in a new category: dwarf planet. Not "failed planet" — a distinct, legitimate classification of its own.

What Actually Changed in 2006

The real trigger wasn't Pluto at all — it was the 2005 discovery of a new object in the outer solar system, initially believed to be larger than Pluto. That object was later named Eris, and its discovery forced a decision astronomers had been avoiding for years: if Pluto is a planet, is Eris one too? What about the dozens of similar objects being found in the same region?

The problem with no rules at all

Without a clear definition, the solar system's planet count could have kept climbing indefinitely as telescopes found more Kuiper Belt objects of similar size. The IAU's 2006 vote wasn't really about demoting Pluto specifically — it was about drawing a consistent, defensible line so "planet" meant something precise.

Meet the Dwarf Planets

Five objects currently hold official dwarf planet status, and several more are strong candidates awaiting formal confirmation.

Beyond these five, objects like Sedna, Quaoar, Gonggong, and Orcus are widely treated as dwarf planets by astronomers, even where formal IAU confirmation is still pending.

Why It's Still Debated

The 2006 decision has never been fully settled. Some planetary scientists — including Alan Stern, the principal investigator of NASA's New Horizons mission to Pluto — argue the "cleared its neighborhood" criterion is poorly defined, since by a strict reading even Earth and Jupiter share their orbital zones with some asteroids. Petitions and counter-arguments have circulated for nearly two decades, and the debate resurfaces reliably whenever Pluto is in the news.

Whatever side of the debate you land on, the current, official classification remains unchanged since 2006: Pluto is a dwarf planet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Pluto still a planet in 2026?

No. Pluto has been classified as a dwarf planet since 2006, when the International Astronomical Union formally defined the term "planet" and Pluto did not meet all three criteria. This classification has not changed since.

Why isn't Pluto considered a planet?

Pluto fails one of the IAU's three planet criteria: it has not "cleared its neighborhood" of other objects. Pluto orbits within the Kuiper Belt alongside thousands of similar icy bodies, some nearly its own size, rather than gravitationally dominating its orbital zone the way the 8 recognized planets do.

What triggered Pluto's reclassification?

The 2005 discovery of Eris, an object in the outer solar system initially believed to be larger and more massive than Pluto, forced astronomers to decide: either declare Eris (and likely several other similar objects) a planet too, or create a clearer definition that placed both in a new category. The IAU chose the second option in 2006.

How many dwarf planets are there?

The IAU formally recognizes five: Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, and Ceres. Several other Kuiper Belt objects, including Sedna, Quaoar, Gonggong, and Orcus, are widely considered likely dwarf planets by astronomers even though they have not all been formally confirmed.
