How Far Is Mars From Earth Right Now?

There is no single answer — Mars is never the same distance away twice. Here's the real range, why it swings so wildly, and how to check today's exact number.

"How far away is Mars?" sounds like it should have one answer. It doesn't — and the difference between the closest and farthest possible distances is bigger than most people expect: roughly a seven-fold difference, depending entirely on where Earth and Mars each happen to be in their orbits on a given day.

At its absolute closest, achieved only rarely when the timing lines up with Mars near its closest point to the Sun, the distance drops to around 56 million kilometers. At its farthest — when Earth and Mars sit on opposite sides of the Sun entirely — it stretches past 400 million kilometers, more than seven times as far.

Why the Distance Varies So Much

Two things combine to create this huge range. First, Earth and Mars orbit the Sun at different speeds: Earth takes 365 days to complete a lap, while Mars takes about 687 days. That mismatch means the two planets are constantly changing their relative position, cycling between being on the same side of the Sun and opposite sides roughly every 26 months.

Second, Mars has a noticeably more elliptical orbit than Earth's. Its distance from the Sun itself varies between about 206.6 million km and 249.2 million km over the course of a single Martian year — so even two "closest approaches" a few cycles apart can differ significantly in exact distance, depending on where Mars happens to be along that ellipse when Earth catches up to it.

What "Opposition" Means

Astronomers call the moment of closest approach an opposition — Earth passes directly between the Sun and Mars, putting Mars opposite the Sun in Earth's sky. It rises at sunset, is visible all night, and appears at its largest and brightest as seen from Earth.

Not every opposition is equal

Because Mars's orbit is elliptical, an opposition that happens to fall when Mars is near its closest point to the Sun (called a perihelic opposition) brings the two planets far closer than an opposition falling when Mars is near its farthest point. This is why some years' close approaches make headlines and others barely get mentioned.

Why This Distance Matters for Missions

The distance to Mars directly determines how long a radio signal takes to arrive — anywhere from about 3 minutes at closest approach to over 22 minutes at the farthest point. This is why Mars rovers like Curiosity and Perseverance cannot be driven in real time; every command has to be pre-planned and sent as a batch, since a live joystick response is physically impossible at that range.

It also determines launch windows. Missions to Mars are only sent during specific weeks every 26 months, timed so the spacecraft can use the shortest, most fuel-efficient path as the two planets align — not whenever it happens to be convenient on Earth.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far is Mars from Earth right now?

The distance changes constantly, ranging from about 56 million km at closest approach to about 401 million km at the farthest point. SatFleet Live's Deep Space tracker shows the real, current distance, calculated live from NASA/JPL data.

Why does the distance to Mars vary so much?

Both planets orbit the Sun at different speeds and distances, and Mars's orbit is noticeably more elliptical than Earth's. Earth completes an orbit in 365 days while Mars takes 687 days, so the two are constantly changing their relative position, sometimes on the same side of the Sun and sometimes on opposite sides.

What is a Mars opposition?

An opposition happens when Earth passes between the Sun and Mars, placing Mars at its closest and brightest as seen from Earth. Oppositions occur roughly every 26 months, though the exact closest distance varies each time because Mars's elliptical orbit means it isn't always at the same point when the opposition happens.

How long does light take to reach Mars?

Light travel time to Mars ranges from about 3 minutes at closest approach to over 22 minutes at the farthest point, which is why real-time control of Mars rovers is impossible and every command has to be pre-planned.
