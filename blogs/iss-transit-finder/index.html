The short answer

An ISS transit is the moment the International Space Station passes in front of the Sun or the Moon as seen from one spot on Earth. It lasts about half a second to two seconds and is only visible from a path on the ground roughly 4 to 15 km wide, so it has to be predicted for an exact place. What is a satellite transit?

A satellite transit happens when a satellite lines up exactly between you and the Sun or the Moon. Both discs are about half a degree wide in the sky, roughly 30 to 33 arcminutes. The ISS, 109 metres across and about 420 km up, looks around 50 arcseconds wide when it is overhead: less than a thirtieth of the disc. Against the Sun or a bright Moon it shows up as a sharp dark silhouette, solar panels included.

The line-up only works along a narrow band. The Sun is 150 million km away and the Moon about 384,000 km, while the station is a few hundred kilometres up, so moving just a few kilometres sideways is enough for the station to slide off the disc. Picture the lines that run from the edges of the Sun or Moon through the station and down to the ground: everything between them is the visible path, and the middle of it is the centre line.

Like a solar eclipse, the transit is longest on the centre line and shortens towards the edges, where the station only clips the rim of the disc. Unlike an eclipse, it is over in about a second.

How to find an ISS transit near you

  1. Open the finder. Go to Next Passes and choose the Sun & Moon transits tab. It works the same in the browser and in the SatFleet Live apps.
  2. Set your location. Allow location access or pick your spot on the map. Until you do, the finder shows results for Madrid and says so.
  3. Choose satellites and distance. Tick the ISS, Tiangong and Hubble (all three are on by default) and choose how far you would travel: 25, 50, 100 or 200 km.
  4. Wait a few seconds. The finder loads the latest orbits and searches the next 7 days. A progress bar shows how far it has got.
  5. Pick a transit and go to the centre line. Each card shows where the centre line is from you and how far. Open in Google Maps takes you to the closest point on it.
  6. Check again the day before. Press Recalculate the day before and shortly before you set up. Orbits change, and the path can move by kilometres.

If nothing falls within your radius, the finder tells you where the nearest one is and offers to search 200 km. How many you get depends on your latitude, the season and how much of the week the Sun and Moon are above your horizon, so widening the radius usually turns up more.

How to read a transit prediction

Each transit is a card, with the most important information first.

At the centre line

The big time is when the satellite crosses the middle of the disc as seen from the closest point on the centre line, in your local time to a tenth of a second, with UTC underneath. Below it you get the duration, the path width and where the Sun or Moon will be in your sky (height above the horizon and compass direction), so you can check that no building or hill is in the way. For the Sun, a red safety warning always comes first.

The map

  • You: your location.
  • Closest point: where the centre line passes nearest to you. This is where the big time applies.
  • Centre line: the track where the satellite crosses the exact middle of the disc.
  • Visible path: the whole strip from which you see a transit at all.

The card also says whether you are already inside the path, and if not, how many kilometres to go and in which compass direction.

The drawing of the crossing

A small drawing shows the satellite's track across the disc: through the centre, and along two side chords (A and B) part of the way to each edge of the path, each with its own duration. If you are inside the path, your own chord is drawn too. Choose Up = zenith to see it the way it looks to your eye or camera, or North up for a telescope without a diagonal. Entry and exit points are given as position angles, in degrees round the disc from north through east.

From where you are now

This tells you what you would see without moving: a transit, if you are inside the path, or a close pass, with how far from the centre of the disc the satellite goes by. It also gives the exact moment of closest approach from your spot and the satellite's height and direction in the sky.

Equipment details

For photographers there is a table comparing your spot with the centre line: angular separation, altitude and azimuth of the satellite and the Sun or Moon, parallactic angle, angular speed, speeds across and along the line of sight, direction of motion, and whether the satellite is lit by the Sun or in Earth's shadow. It also suggests the slowest shutter speed that keeps the outline sharp and how many video frames the crossing will take at 30, 60 and 120 frames per second.

Apparent sizes and times are approximate, for a satellite straight overhead. Lower in the sky the satellite is farther away, so it looks smaller and crosses more slowly, up to about two or three seconds.

The ISS is the easiest: biggest, brightest and seen from most of the populated world. Tiangong is half the size but just as quick, and its lower inclination means fewer chances from northern Europe or Canada. Hubble is a challenge for experienced imagers: never above the horizon from the UK, and so small that a short focal length shows only a dot. Read more in our Tiangong guide.

Sun transits and Moon transits

Never look at the Sun without a certified filter

Even a glance through a camera, binoculars or telescope can blind you instantly. Use eclipse glasses meeting ISO 12312-2 for your eyes, and a certified full-aperture solar filter fitted to the front of any optics before you point them anywhere near the Sun.

Solar transits are the most dramatic: the outline is crisp against a bright, even disc, sometimes with sunspots in the frame. They happen in daylight, so you can plan them for an afternoon.

Lunar transits need no solar filter and look best when the Moon is well lit. The finder warns you when the Moon is almost new and barely visible, and when the sky will still be bright enough to wash it out. Because the Moon is often up in daylight too, not every lunar transit happens at night.

How to see and photograph an ISS transit

  • Go to the centre lineThe crossing is longest there and the timing least sensitive to small errors.
  • Use a synchronised clockSet your phone to network time. The prediction is given to a tenth of a second.
  • Start early, stop lateRecord video or a burst from several seconds before until a few seconds after the predicted time.
  • Use a long focal lengthAround 1,000 mm or more, on a telescope or a long lens with a teleconverter, shows the solar panels.
  • Keep the shutter fastUse at least the shutter speed the finder suggests; raise the ISO rather than slow down.
  • Frame the whole discYou may not know exactly where the satellite will enter, so keep the full Sun or Moon in view.

Afterwards, stack the frames that show the satellite into one image to show its whole track across the disc. The finder's video frame count tells you how many frames to expect, which is handy for checking you caught it.

How SatFleet Live calculates transits

Everything runs in your browser, in a background worker so the page stays responsive. Your location is used there for the calculation.

  1. Fresh orbits. The finder loads the latest orbital elements for the three satellites, the same data SatFleet Live refreshes from Space-Track every 2 hours. See what a TLE is.
  2. Positions. Satellite positions come from the SGP4 model (satellite.js). The Sun and Moon come from Astronomy Engine, calculated exactly every 10 minutes and interpolated in between, with an error below 0.1 arcseconds.
  3. Search. It scans the next 7 days for moments when a satellite and the Sun or Moon are close together in the sky and both above the horizon, with a margin generous enough to cover anywhere within 200 km of you.
  4. Centre line. For each candidate it projects the line from the centre of the Sun or Moon through the satellite down to the ground, sampled every 0.2 seconds. That moving point is the centre line.
  5. Width and duration. It works out how long the satellite takes to cross the disc on the centre line, then steps sideways until the satellite only touches the edge of the disc, which gives the path width.
  6. Your view. Finally it calculates everything as seen from your own spot and from the centre line, which fills the equipment table.

Results are kept in your browser and recalculated automatically when newer orbit data arrives, at least twice a day, or when you move more than 1 km.

How accurate are the predictions?

With fresh orbit data a day or so ahead, a prediction is usually right to well under a second and to within about a kilometre of the path. Further ahead it drifts: the thin upper atmosphere slows the satellites down, more so when solar activity is high, and the ISS and Tiangong fire their engines from time to time to raise their orbits or dodge debris. A single burn can move a transit by seconds and its path by many kilometres.

That is why the finder marks transits more than 48 hours away as less reliable. Use them to plan, then recalculate the day before and on the day. For a once-off trip, it is worth checking the live map for the station's current position too.

Frequently asked questions

What is an ISS transit?

The moment the International Space Station passes in front of the Sun or the Moon as seen from a particular spot on Earth. The station appears as a small dark silhouette crossing the disc. It is only visible from a narrow strip of ground a few kilometres wide.

How long does an ISS transit last?

About half a second when the ISS passes nearly overhead, and up to about two or three seconds when it is low in the sky and therefore farther away. It is longest on the centre line and gets shorter towards the edges of the path. SatFleet Live gives the exact duration for each transit.

How wide is the ISS transit path?

Usually a few kilometres to about 15 kilometres. It is narrowest when the Sun or Moon is high and the station is close, and wider when they are low in the sky. Outside the path the ISS passes beside the disc instead of in front of it.

How do I find ISS transits near me?

Open the Sun & Moon transits tab in Next Passes on SatFleet Live, set your location and choose a search radius of 25, 50, 100 or 200 km. It lists every transit of the Sun or Moon by the ISS, Tiangong and Hubble within that distance over the next 7 days, with a map of where to stand.

Is the SatFleet Live transit finder free?

Which satellites can cross the Sun or Moon visibly?

Only large, low satellites show a recognisable outline. The finder covers the ISS (about 109 m across), the Chinese space station Tiangong (about 55 m) and the Hubble Space Telescope (about 13 m). Hubble is so small that you need a telescope or a long telephoto lens to make it out.

Can I see an ISS transit with the naked eye?

It is very hard. The ISS covers less than a thirtieth of the Moon's width and is gone in about a second. Most people watch through a telescope or record with a camera on a long lens. Never look at the Sun, even briefly, without a certified solar filter.

Is it safe to watch an ISS transit of the Sun?

Only with proper protection. Use eclipse glasses that meet ISO 12312-2 for your eyes, and a certified full-aperture solar filter on the front of any telescope, binoculars or camera lens. Never point unfiltered optics at the Sun.

How accurate are ISS transit predictions?

With fresh orbit data a day or so ahead, a prediction is usually right to well under a second and to within about a kilometre of the path. Further ahead it drifts, because drag and engine burns change the orbit. Anything more than two or three days away is a rough guide; SatFleet Live marks transits more than 48 hours away as less reliable. Recalculate the day before.

What is the difference between a transit and a close pass?

From inside the path the satellite crosses the disc: that is a transit. From outside it the satellite passes beside the Sun or Moon: that is a close pass. SatFleet Live shows which one you will see from your current spot and by how much it misses.

Can Tiangong and Hubble be seen from my country?

It depends on your latitude. Tiangong's orbit is inclined 41.5 degrees and Hubble's 28.5 degrees, so they pass high overhead only nearer the equator. Hubble never rises above the horizon north of about 50 degrees latitude, which rules out the UK, and Tiangong is out of reach beyond about 60 degrees. The ISS, at 51.6 degrees, reaches much further.
