Moon Phase Tonight: What You'll See and Why

The Moon isn't changing shape, and Earth's shadow has nothing to do with it. What you see tonight depends entirely on where the Moon happens to be standing relative to the Sun and to you.

Almost everyone has looked up at a crescent Moon and half-remembered an explanation involving Earth's shadow. It is the most widely believed wrong answer in astronomy, and the real one is simpler and more satisfying.

Why the Shape Changes

Here is the whole thing in one sentence: half the Moon is always lit, and we see that lit half from a different angle each night.

The Sun illuminates one hemisphere of the Moon permanently, just as it illuminates one hemisphere of Earth. What changes is our viewing angle. As the Moon travels around Earth over roughly 29.5 days, we move from looking at the unlit side, to seeing it edge-on, to seeing the lit face almost head-on, and back again.

The lit half always faces the Sun. Only our viewing angle changes as the Moon goes around.

The shadow myth

Earth's shadow does fall on the Moon occasionally — that is a lunar eclipse, and it happens a couple of times a year at most, lasting hours. Phases happen every single night. They are not the same phenomenon.

Those first two numbers are worth a moment. The Moon completes its orbit in 27.3 days, but a full phase cycle takes 29.53. The difference exists because Earth moves too: by the time the Moon has gone once around, Earth has travelled along its own orbit, so the Moon needs a little extra to line up with the Sun again.

Waxing or Waning? A Quick Test

Waxing means growing, waning means shrinking. Telling them apart at a glance is easy once you know the trick — though the trick depends on where you live.

From the northern hemisphere

Lit on the right, it is waxing — heading toward full. Lit on the left, it is waning — heading toward new.

The classic memory aid in English uses the letters themselves: a crescent shaped like a D is waxing (Developing), and one shaped like a C is waning (Closing). In Spanish and French the same shapes mean the opposite, which is a nice reminder that these are mnemonics, not physics.

From the southern hemisphere

Reverse both. Lit on the left means waxing; lit on the right means waning.

Why It Looks Flipped in Australia

This surprises people who move hemispheres, and the explanation is disarmingly simple: you are standing upside down relative to each other.

The Moon has not changed. But an observer in Sydney is oriented roughly opposite to one in Madrid, so the same lunar face arrives rotated by about 180 degrees. A crescent lit on the right from Europe is lit on the left from Australia, and the dark patches that northern observers call the Man in the Moon appear upside down — which is part of why other cultures see a rabbit there instead.

Near the equator

From tropical latitudes a crescent can appear to lie on its back, horns pointing up, like a bowl. It is sometimes called a wet moon. Same Moon, same phase, different angle of view.

What's Worth Looking At Tonight

A common assumption is that full moon is the best night to look. It is actually one of the worst, if you have any kind of optics.

At full moon the Sun is directly behind you, so the surface is flatly lit and shadows vanish. Craters lose their depth and the whole disc turns into a bright, washed-out circle. It is beautiful with the naked eye and disappointing through binoculars.

The interesting nights are near first or last quarter. Along the terminator, the Sun sits low on the lunar horizon and throws long shadows across crater rims and mountain ranges. Mountains that are invisible at full moon suddenly have relief. Even ordinary binoculars show it clearly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What moon phase is it tonight?

The phase changes every night across a cycle of roughly 29.5 days, so there is no single fixed answer. The SatFleet Live Moon tracker shows the current phase on a live 3D Moon, with the illuminated fraction as a percentage and the terminator line drawn in its real position for this moment.

Why does the Moon change shape?

The Moon does not change shape, and it is not Earth's shadow either. Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun. As the Moon orbits Earth over about 29.5 days, we see that lit half from different angles — sometimes almost fully, sometimes edge-on, sometimes not at all. The phase is simply how much of the sunlit side happens to be facing us.

How long is a full lunar cycle?

29.53 days, known as a synodic month. This is slightly longer than the 27.3 days the Moon takes to orbit Earth, because Earth also moves around the Sun during that time, so the Moon needs a little extra travel to return to the same alignment.

What is the difference between waxing and waning?

Waxing means the lit portion is growing, on the way from new moon to full moon. Waning means it is shrinking, from full moon back to new. A simple check in the northern hemisphere: if the lit edge is on the right, it is waxing; on the left, it is waning. In the southern hemisphere this is reversed.

Why does the Moon look upside down in Australia?

Because observers in the southern hemisphere are, relative to those in the north, standing the other way up. The Moon itself is unchanged, but a crescent that appears lit on the right from Europe appears lit on the left from Australia, and the familiar dark patches look rotated. Near the equator, a crescent can appear to sit like a bowl with the horns pointing upward.

Can I see past and future moon phases?

Yes. The Moon tracker includes a playback timeline that lets you move the clock to another date, with the lighting, the terminator and the phase indicator all recalculated for that moment. Scrubbing through a month shows the entire cycle play out in a few seconds.

Is the best time to observe the Moon at full moon?

No, despite the assumption. At full moon the surface is lit head-on and shadows disappear, flattening the view. First and last quarter are far better for detail: the Sun sits low along the terminator and casts long shadows that reveal crater depth and mountain relief, visible even with basic binoculars.
