Every spacecraft NASA has ever launched eventually falls into one of two categories: it orbits something, or it is on its way out. Voyager 1 is the most extreme example of the second kind — launched in 1977 to fly past Jupiter and Saturn, it never stopped afterward, and it is not going to.

How Far Away It Is Right Now

As of 2026, Voyager 1 sits at roughly 25.6 billion kilometers from Earth — about 171 times the distance between the Earth and the Sun. That number is not fixed; it grows by more than 1.4 million kilometers every single day, since Voyager 1 continues moving outward at around 17 kilometers per second.

Why we don't give you a single fixed number

Any exact distance printed in an article is already slightly wrong by the time you read it. At that distance, radio signals — travelling at the speed of light — take roughly 23 to 24 hours to make the one-way trip. Every command NASA sends has to wait nearly two full days for a round-trip confirmation.

The Journey So Far

September 1977

Voyager 1 launches from Cape Canaveral — technically after Voyager 2, but on a faster trajectory that let it arrive at Jupiter first.

March 1979

Jupiter flyby. Voyager 1 discovers active volcanoes on the moon Io — the first ever found beyond Earth.

November 1980

Saturn flyby, and Voyager 1's last planetary encounter. Its trajectory was deliberately bent to study Titan up close, sending it out of the plane of the solar system for good.

February 1990

From nearly 6 billion km away, Voyager 1 turns its camera back toward home and captures the "Pale Blue Dot" — Earth as a single pixel suspended in sunlight.

August 2012

Voyager 1 crosses the heliopause, leaving the bubble of solar particles that surrounds our solar system, and enters interstellar space.

Today

Still transmitting, still over 47 years into its mission, with no planetary targets left — only distance.

What "Interstellar Space" Actually Means

It is a common misconception that Voyager 1 has "left the solar system." In the sense that matters for a headline, it has: it has crossed the heliopause, the boundary where the constant outward stream of particles from the Sun (the solar wind) is overpowered by the thin material drifting between stars.

Gravitationally, though, Voyager 1 is still very much bound to the Sun. It would need to travel through the Oort Cloud — a distant shell of icy bodies that may extend up to two light-years out — before truly leaving the Sun's gravitational influence, a journey that would take roughly 30,000 years at its current speed.

Is It Still Working?

Yes, but its power is fading. Voyager 1 runs on a radioisotope generator that loses about 4 watts of output every year as its plutonium decays. NASA has been shutting down onboard science instruments one at a time to stretch the mission further, with the goal of keeping at least one instrument transmitting into the 2030s.

Even after its instruments eventually go silent, Voyager 1's physical journey does not stop. It will keep drifting outward for millions of years, carrying its Golden Record — a phonograph disc of sounds and images from Earth — long after anyone on this planet remembers building it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far is Voyager 1 from Earth right now?

As of 2026, Voyager 1 is roughly 25.6 billion kilometers (about 171 Astronomical Units) from the Sun, and moving farther away by more than 1.4 million km every day. The exact, continuously updated distance is shown live on SatFleet Live's Deep Space tracker.

Is Voyager 1 still transmitting data?

Yes, though its power supply is fading roughly 4 watts per year as its plutonium generator decays. NASA has progressively shut down onboard science instruments to conserve power, aiming to keep at least one instrument running into the 2030s.

Has Voyager 1 left the solar system?

Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause into interstellar space in August 2012, meaning it has left the bubble of charged particles from the Sun. It has not left the solar system in the gravitational sense, since it remains bound by the Sun's gravity and will take tens of thousands of years to reach the Oort Cloud's outer edge.
