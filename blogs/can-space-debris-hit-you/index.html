Can Space Debris Hit You?

Thousands of tonnes of satellites and rocket stages have fallen back to Earth since 1957. Here is how many people they have actually hit, what the real odds are, and what to do if a piece ever lands near you.

Quick answer

Technically yes, but it is extraordinarily unlikely. In more than 65 years of spaceflight, only one person is known to have been touched by falling space debris, and she was unhurt. The chance of a specific person being hit by a given re-entry is often put at less than one in a trillion — far lower than being struck by lightning.

Every week, old satellites, spent rocket stages and fragments of space debris fall back into the atmosphere. Most of them vanish in a streak of light. A few leave pieces that reach the ground. So it is a fair question: can space debris hit you? The honest answer is yes, in theory — but the numbers behind that "yes" are reassuring.

The Real Odds, Compared

Risk figures for falling debris are easy to misread, because two very different numbers get quoted. One is the chance that anyone at all on Earth is hurt by a re-entry. The other is the chance that you are. The second is the one that matters to you, and it is vanishingly small.

Put simply: the whole planet shares a small risk with every large re-entry, and that risk is divided among eight billion people spread over a surface that is mostly water. Your personal share is about as close to zero as a real number gets.

Has Anyone Ever Been Hit?

Only once, as far as anyone knows — and a handful of near misses show that pieces do reach inhabited areas.

January 1997 — Tulsa, Oklahoma

Lottie Williams was out walking when a light, scorched piece of woven material brushed her shoulder. It was later linked to a Delta II rocket stage that re-entered over Texas the same night, where a large steel propellant tank also landed near a farmhouse. She was not hurt, and remains the only person known to have been touched by space debris.

May 2020 — Côte d'Ivoire

Debris from a Chinese Long March 5B core stage was reported in villages after an uncontrolled re-entry. There were no injuries. We cover how that fall zone was predicted in Where Will Space Debris Fall?

July 2022 — New South Wales, Australia

Farmers found charred fragments in their fields, later identified as parts of the unpressurised trunk section of a SpaceX Dragon capsule.

March 2024 — Naples, Florida

A small metal support part from a battery pallet discarded by the International Space Station punched through the roof of a family home. NASA confirmed where it came from. Nobody was injured, but it became the best-documented case of space debris hitting a house.

What Survives Re-entry

Objects falling from orbit hit the atmosphere at around 28,000 km/h. Friction and compression heat them to thousands of degrees, and most break apart at around 70 to 80 km of altitude. What happens next depends on what they are made of.

Aluminium, the main material in most satellites, melts at about 660 °C and usually disappears completely. Titanium, stainless steel and composite-wrapped tanks are far tougher. That is why the pieces found on the ground are so often propellant tanks, pressure spheres, engine parts and other dense, heat-resistant components.

Size matters most

Small satellites and debris fragments almost always burn up entirely. The objects that can leave surviving pieces are the big, heavy ones — above all spent rocket bodies, which can weigh several tonnes. For large objects, analysts commonly estimate that somewhere between 10 and 40 percent of the original mass may survive.

Why Being Hit Is So Rare

Three things work in your favour. First, most of the planet is empty of people: about 71 percent of Earth's surface is ocean, and much of the land is desert, forest or ice. Second, an object can only fall beneath its own orbit, so anyone living at latitudes higher than its inclination is completely out of reach. Third, the surviving pieces that do reach the ground land in a narrow strip, not a wide shower.

Combine those and the result is what the record shows: tens of thousands of objects have re-entered since the start of the space age, and the only person known to have been touched walked away with a story.

The Rules That Keep the Risk Low

Space agencies do not leave this entirely to luck. NASA's debris standard requires that an uncontrolled re-entry carry no more than a 1 in 10,000 chance of injuring anyone anywhere. When a spacecraft would exceed that, it must be steered down into a remote area of ocean on purpose — a controlled re-entry — or redesigned.

Manufacturers increasingly practise design for demise: choosing materials and layouts so that nothing survives the fall. Large constellations such as Starlink are built this way, so that thousands of satellites can be retired without adding risk on the ground.

What to Do If You Find Space Debris

Don't touch it. Fragments can have sharp edges and some may carry toxic propellant residue.

Photograph it and note the exact location, ideally with GPS coordinates from your phone.

Report it to your local police or emergency services, who can pass it to the national space or civil protection authority.

Don't keep or sell it. Under international space law, the object still belongs to the country that launched it, and that country is liable for any damage it caused.

Frequently Asked Questions

Has anyone ever been hit by space debris?

Only one person is known to have been touched by falling space debris: Lottie Williams, who was brushed on the shoulder by a light piece of rocket material in Tulsa, Oklahoma, in January 1997. She was not hurt. No death or serious injury from re-entering space debris has ever been confirmed.

What are the odds of being hit by space junk?

Extremely low. The chance of a specific person being hit by debris from a given re-entry is often quoted as less than one in a trillion. By comparison, the lifetime odds of being struck by lightning in the US are about one in 15,000.

Does space debris burn up on re-entry?

Most of it does. Objects break apart at around 70 to 80 km of altitude and the majority of their mass melts or vaporises. Large objects made with heat-resistant materials, such as titanium or steel tanks from rocket stages, can leave surviving fragments that reach the ground.

Has space debris ever hit a house?

Yes. In March 2024 a small metal part from a battery pallet discarded from the International Space Station crashed through the roof of a house in Naples, Florida. NASA confirmed its origin. Nobody was injured.

What should I do if I find space debris?

Do not touch it, as some fragments can carry toxic propellant residue or have sharp edges. Photograph it, note the location and report it to your local police or emergency services. Under international law the object still belongs to the country that launched it.
