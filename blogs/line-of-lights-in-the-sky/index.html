A line of lights in the sky moving slowly and silently in single file, with no blinking, is almost always a Starlink train: a group of SpaceX satellites launched in the previous days that have not yet spread out. If it was a single bright, steady light crossing the sky in a few minutes, it was most likely the International Space Station. Blinking lights point to a plane, and a streak that lasted only a second was a meteor.

This guide helps you work out what you saw from four simple clues: shape, speed, brightness and duration. If it was a satellite, there is a good chance you can see it again, and Next Passes shows when it will next be visible from your location.

What Was That Light in the Sky? A Quick Checklist

If you are still not sure, run through these questions in order. The first one that matches is usually the answer.

  1. Did it blink or have red and green lights? It was almost certainly a plane. Satellites and the ISS shine with a steady light because they only reflect sunlight.
  2. Was it over in a second or two? That is a meteor. Nothing in orbit crosses the sky that quickly.
  3. Was it several lights in a row, moving together at the same speed? A Starlink train, especially if there was a SpaceX launch in the last few days.
  4. Was it a single, very bright light crossing the sky in a few minutes? Most likely the ISS. Fainter single lights are other satellites.
  5. Did a light flash brightly for a few seconds and then fade? A satellite flare, sunlight glinting off a flat surface.
  6. Was it a glowing, spreading cloud or spiral, close to dusk or dawn? A rocket after launch or an upper stage venting fuel.
  7. Was it a slow, fiery cluster breaking into pieces? Re-entering space debris.
  8. Was it orange, flickering and drifting with the wind, or hovering in a pattern? Sky lanterns or a drone show.

Is a Line of Lights in the Sky a Starlink Train?

In most cases, yes. When SpaceX launches a batch of Starlink satellites, they are released close together in a low orbit. For the next few days they travel in a tight line, one behind the other, while they use their thrusters to climb towards their final altitude. Seen from the ground, this looks like a string of lights in the sky moving silently in single file, sometimes described as a "train" or a "string of pearls".

The train is most visible in the first days after a launch, when the satellites are low, close together and oriented in a way that reflects more sunlight. As they climb and spread out, the line stretches, breaks up and becomes fainter, until the individual satellites are hard to see with the naked eye. SpaceX has also made its satellites dimmer once they reach their working orbit.

A common detail that confirms a Starlink train: the lights may disappear one after another at the same point in the sky. That is not the satellites switching off, but each one entering Earth's shadow in turn.

Was It the International Space Station?

If you saw one light that was brighter than any star, moved steadily across a large part of the sky and took a few minutes to do it, it was very probably the ISS. It does not blink, it makes no sound and it usually travels from a westerly direction towards the east. It can also fade out before reaching the horizon when it enters Earth's shadow.

The ISS is usually visible from the same region on several days in a row, so you will probably have more chances soon. You can check where the ISS is right now on the live map, and Next Passes will tell you its next visible pass over you.

Why Do Satellites Look Like Moving Lights in the Sky?

Satellites have no lights of their own that you can see from the ground. What you see is sunlight reflected off their bodies and solar panels. That is why they appear only at certain times: your sky must be dark, but the satellite, hundreds of kilometres up, must still be in sunlight. These conditions are met in the hours after sunset and before sunrise, and in summer at higher latitudes they can last much of the night.

The same physics explains why brightness changes during a pass and why some satellites flash. Our guide Why Are Satellites So Bright? explains reflection angles, flares and the magnitude scale astronomers use to measure brightness.

What is a satellite flare?

A flare happens when a flat, shiny surface on a satellite, such as a solar panel or antenna, reflects sunlight directly towards you. The satellite suddenly becomes much brighter for a few seconds, then fades back to normal or disappears. The best-known flares came from the first generation of Iridium satellites, which have since been retired, but many other satellites still produce brief glints.

Was It a Rocket After a Launch?

Some of the most striking sightings come from rockets. When a launch happens shortly before sunrise or after sunset, the exhaust plume high in the atmosphere is lit by the Sun while the ground below is already dark. The result is a glowing cloud, sometimes shaped like a jellyfish or a fan, that spreads slowly as it moves. It can be seen hundreds of kilometres from the launch site.

Upper stages can create similar displays later, when they vent leftover fuel or fire their engines to leave orbit. Depending on how the stage is spinning, the vented fuel can form a glowing spiral or a fuzzy disc. If you saw something like this, check recent launches for a mission around that time. How Can You Tell a Plane From a Satellite?

Planes carry navigation lights that satellites do not: a red light on the left wingtip, a green light on the right and white strobes that flash at regular intervals. If any light on the object blinked, it was not a satellite. Planes can also turn, change speed and are often audible, while satellites follow a smooth, straight path in complete silence.

Was It a Meteor or a Fireball?

A meteor is a grain or small piece of rock burning up as it enters the atmosphere at tens of kilometres per second. It appears as a quick streak that is usually over in less than a second or two. An unusually bright meteor, brighter than Venus, is called a fireball, and it may break into several pieces or leave a short glowing trail.

Meteors are much more common during meteor showers, such as the Perseids in August and the Geminids in December, but you can see them on any clear night. If you saw a bright fireball, you can report it to organisations such as the American Meteor Society, which collect reports from witnesses to work out its path.

Meteor or re-entering space debris?

Space debris falling back to Earth, such as an old rocket stage or a dead satellite, also burns up in the atmosphere, but it enters much more slowly than a meteor. The result is a slow, fiery procession of fragments, often orange or yellow with long tails, that can take a minute or more to cross the sky. This is one of the few situations where a genuine line of fireballs appears. Our satellite re-entry tracker guide explains how these events are predicted.

Are Moving Lights in the Sky UFOs?

The vast majority of strange lights in the sky have ordinary explanations. Since large satellite constellations began launching, Starlink trains have become one of the most common causes of reports, along with rocket plumes, the ISS, planes, sky lanterns and drone shows.

How to Identify It Next Time

A few habits make any future sighting much easier to identify:

  • Note the time as precisely as you can, and the direction the lights came from and went to.
  • Count the seconds it stays visible, and watch for blinking.
  • Check a tracker straight away. The SatFleet Live map shows satellites in real time, so you can see which ones are above you at that moment, and Next Passes lists the visible passes coming up.
  • Check recent launches. A Starlink launch in the last few days, or a launch that evening, explains many sightings.
