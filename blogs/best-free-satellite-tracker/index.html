What to look for in a free satellite tracker

A satellite tracker turns orbital data into a position you can see. Six things separate a good one from a frustrating one:

  • Fresh orbital dataOrbits drift with drag and manoeuvres, so old data means wrong positions.
  • A big catalogueThousands of satellites, not a short list of famous ones.
  • Pass predictionsWhen something flies over your location, and how bright it will be.
  • No barriersNo forced sign-up, no paywall on the basics.
  • Reach beyond Earth orbitThe Moon, Mars and deep-space probes, if you care about them.
  • Phone supportA site that works on mobile, ideally with an app.

How fresh is the data?

Satellite positions come from a TLE (two-line element set), a compact description of an orbit. TLE data ages, which is why update frequency matters. Some guides, including an earlier version of this one, said SatFleet Live updates every 48 hours. That is out of date: orbital data now refreshes every 2 hours.

Tips for spotting satellites

  • Go out around dusk or dawn. The sky is dark but the satellite is still lit by the Sun. Satellites in Earth's shadow cannot be seen.
  • Favour high passes. A pass that climbs high in the sky is brighter and easier to follow than one skimming the horizon.
  • Know your IDs. Every satellite has a NORAD catalogue number: ISS 25544, Hubble 20580, Tiangong 48274.
  • Pair the map with Next Passes. The map shows where something is now, Next Passes tells you when it is above you.
  • Catch Starlink trains early. A train is a line of satellites still close together after a launch, so look in the days after one. satellitemap.space and Find Starlink both have tools for it.
  • Treat far-off times as estimates. Space stations adjust their orbits, so check again the day before.

How often is the satellite data updated?

SatFleet Live refreshes the orbital data (TLE-style elements) for active satellites every 2 hours from Space-Track. Debris refreshes every 12 hours, re-entry predictions and Moon, Mars and Deep Space data every 6 hours, and launch data every 5 to 60 minutes depending on how close the next launch is. Positions are then calculated continuously in your browser with the SGP4 model. For comparison, Orbital Radar states that it refreshes orbital elements multiple times a day, and satellitemap.space does not state a fixed interval on the pages we checked.

Why are some satellites missing from the map?

The Active view only includes satellites that are still in orbit, appear on CelesTrak's active list (or launched in the last 30 days) and have valid orbital elements from the last 30 days. Objects with incomplete data are skipped instead of drawn in the wrong place, and classified satellites are not in the public catalogue.
